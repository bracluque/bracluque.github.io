A guide for research teams

Coding at Scale: LLM-Assisted Extraction for Systematic Reviews

Treat the model like a new research assistant, not a script: it needs a codebook, blind double-coding, adjudication, and measured agreement. Skip any of those and you're trusting output nobody checked.

Case study — IDB Extended School Day Review Guide + full pipeline, self-contained
§1

Overview

Goal

Produce structured, auditable data from a large PDF corpus for a systematic review — at a scale and consistency manual RA coding can't match.

Why

An LLM states a wrong number as confidently as a right one. Treat it as an unsupervised coder: it needs a codebook, blind double-coding, adjudication, and a measured reliability score, or errors compound silently at scale.

Input

Source PDFs, a codebook (one Markdown file, written before any extraction — see §7), and Claude Code. Not "an LLM environment" generically: the orchestrator/worker split in §2.9 runs on Claude Code's subagent dispatch specifically, and that's not something you can swap in a different chat interface for.

Process

Two independent coders extract the same paper blind → a reconciler adjudicates disagreements against the source PDF, not against either draft → before scaling, a reliability pilot scores agreement between the two coders → anything the model can't support is flagged, not guessed.

Output

One Markdown record per paper, evidence quotes inline (§4). Paste it into the review's master sheet, or use it as an independent baseline against a consultant's manual extraction of the same paper. It can also export semi-automatically into a spreadsheet — faster, but still budget time to verify it.

Who it's for

Teams with a large corpus, fields that have a clear codeable answer, and time to pilot before running the full set — see §6 for the full fit criteria.

Access

Everything is here — this guide, the generalized template, and the complete real ESD pipeline (full codebook, prompts, worker definitions), self-contained at one link — see §8.

Systematic reviews need structured data — sample sizes, effect sizes, p-values, study design — pulled out of hundreds of PDFs. Doing that by hand doesn't scale past a few dozen papers without months of research-assistant time; an LLM does the transcription in a fraction of that time, but only if it's managed like the unsupervised coder it is.

That management is the rest of this guide: a codebook, blind double-coding, adjudication, and a measured reliability score, all before you trust the output at the full corpus.

§2

Design principles

Nine principles, in the order they get used: write the codebook, extract without inferring, require evidence, code twice blind, reconcile against the source, measure agreement, flag honestly, audit by hand, manage context for scale.

2.1 — Foundation

The codebook is the product

Before any paper touches the model, every field needs an unambiguous definition: what counts, what doesn't, how edge cases are coded, what "leave blank" means versus "not applicable." Most extraction failures aren't model failures — they're codebook gaps that the model quietly papered over with a plausible guess. Time spent tightening the codebook is the highest-leverage time in the whole project, and it's cheaper to spend before the first paper than after the five-hundredth.

2.2 — Scope

Extract, don't calculate

The model should transcribe what's on the page — not compute Cohen's d, not infer a p-value from a coefficient and standard error, not resolve ambiguity with a best guess. Every inference step is a place where an error compounds silently and becomes indistinguishable from a fact. Keep calculation as a separate, auditable step downstream of extraction — done by a human or a deterministic script — never folded into the same pass that reads the page.

2.3 — Evidence

Every value needs a receipt

Require a short verbatim quote and a page number attached to every extracted field. This does two things at once: it forces the model to ground its answer in the actual text instead of pattern-matching to what a typical paper in this literature usually reports, and it gives a reviewer something to check in thirty seconds instead of re-reading the paper. A value with no quote behind it is a value you cannot trust — treat "no quote found" as a valid, expected outcome, not a failure to paper over.

2.4 — Independence

Code blind, and code twice

Run two independent extraction passes over every paper, each with zero visibility into the other's answers — the same logic as dual independent screening in a human-run systematic review. Where the two agree, you have real signal. Where they disagree, you've found precisely the ambiguous cases that deserve a closer look, not a coin flip or a tiebreak by whichever ran first.

The two independent coders don't have to both be models. Run the pipeline over a paper a consultant already coded by hand, without either side seeing the other's answers, and reconcile the two — now the pipeline checks the human coder instead of replacing them.

2.5 — Adjudication

Reconcile against the source, not against the drafts

A third pass compares the two independent drafts field by field. Agreements pass through untouched. Disagreements are resolved by re-reading the source PDF — the paper is the arbiter, not whichever draft sounds more confident. The reconciler writes a short report for every paper: what disagreed, what the source actually said, what was decided. That report is your audit trail, and it should be legible to someone who wasn't in the room.

2.6 — Validation

Measure agreement before you trust the pipeline

This is the step most LLM-extraction projects skip, and it's the one that actually tells you whether the pipeline works. Before scaling up, take a sample, run both independent coders over it, and score their agreement the way you'd report inter-rater reliability for two human RAs: accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 per field — or Cohen's kappa where a field is categorical. A field with low agreement isn't a model problem to route around with a better prompt; it's the codebook telling you it's ambiguous. Fix the codebook, then re-test, before you run the other 200 papers through it.

flag Not done on this project

The ESD review did not run this step — no reliability pilot, no accuracy/precision/recall/F1 scorecard was produced before scaling to the full corpus. That's a gap, not a design choice, and the same honesty rule as §2.7 applies to the guide itself: naming the gap is better than quietly implying it happened. It should be standard practice — strongly recommended for any run of this pipeline, including a second pass at this one.

2.7 — Honesty

Flags are a feature

When neither coder can find support for a field, the correct output is an explicit flag for human review — not a blank filled with the modal answer, and not "not reported" as a default written out of habit. A visible gap is honest. A silently guessed value is not, and it's far more expensive to catch later.

2.8 — Oversight

Human spot-checks stay mandatory

Reconciliation reduces error; it does not eliminate it.

From production

In one run, a reconciler discarded the correct independent draft in favor of one that actually described a different paper entirely — and asserted, incorrectly, that it had verified the choice against the source PDF. Nothing internal to the pipeline caught this. A human reviewer did, by noticing the extracted numbers didn't match their memory of the paper.

Build periodic full-dataset diffs and spot audits into the process as a standing practice, not a one-time cleanup step after something looks wrong.

2.9 — Mechanics

Manage context with an orchestrator, not a marathon session

This part is plumbing, not methodology, but it's what makes the rest of this achievable at hundreds of papers: a single long-running session runs out of context after a handful of dense extractions. Split the work instead — a lightweight orchestrator plans and dispatches small batches to fresh-context worker sessions, each of which extracts its batch, writes to disk, and reports back only a short summary, never full file contents. That's what lets one project run to completion instead of restarting every five papers.

## 14. Notes

### 14a. Bulleted notes
- This is an observational study using propensity score matching (PSM) and the Altonji, Elder, and Taber (AET) selection model — not a true experiment.
- Four models compared: (1) OLS without controls, (2) OLS with controls (value-added), (3) PSM nearest-neighbor matching, (4) AET selection model.
- PSM results show null to weakly negative effects; AET results show statistically significant negative effects of sports participation on all three test scores.
- PSM results are arguably the "upper bound" assuming positive selection; AET provides the "lower bound."
- Sports participation is a binary indicator (any school-sponsored sports vs none), not a specific program.
- The study focuses on middle school (8th grade), which is unusual in the sports-academics literature.
- The study does not measure the effect of any specific ESD intervention — it measures a naturally occurring extracurricular participation exposure.
- Sports participation rate is 58% (4,850 participants, 3,000 non-participants).
- All models control for 5th-grade lagged test scores and a rich set of student, family, and school characteristics.
- F0 coded as 2 (non-academic/non-cognitive) rather than 6 (health services) because school-sponsored sports are extracurricular activities within the school, not external health services.

### 14b. Linear prose summary *(copy-paste friendly)*

> This observational study uses ECLS-K data to examine whether middle school sports participation affects academic performance among 7,850 nationally representative 8th-grade students. Using propensity score matching and the AET selection model to account for selection bias, the study finds that the positive association between sports and academics disappears once selection is controlled for, and the AET estimates are consistently negative and significant. This is not a traditional ESD intervention study but rather an analysis of extracurricular sports participation as a naturally occurring exposure. Both PSM (upper bound) and AET (lower bound) results are extracted.

**Abstract:**
> Because large-scale studies repeatedly indicated low reading literacy for many students, a need for interventions fostering reading literacy, such as extracurricular tutoring, has often been emphasized. Several reading promoting programs, suitable for extracurricular tutoring, were developed and shown to be effective in recent years. Moreover, these studies and analyses of extracurricular activities and tutoring yielded findings on learning-supporting characteristics and effects on students' achievement of such extracurricular offers. Nevertheless, little is known to date about the implementation of extracurricular tutoring in practice in Germany, particularly about its participation rates, characteristics, and effects on students' reading literacy. Thus, the present study investigated participation rates of extracurricular tutoring in reading and in German among students in general and among various subgroups between Grades 5 and 7. Furthermore, the study examined general and subject-specific characteristics and effects of extracurricular tutoring on reading literacy. In addition to descriptive analyses of characteristics and participation rates of extracurricular tutoring, effect sizes were calculated for comparing participation rates of subgroups of students. Furthermore, multi-group structural equation models were implemented to investigate average and differential effects of extracurricular tutoring, while controlling for initial reading literacy and other covariates. Moreover, the results revealed no significant effects of extracurricular tutoring on students' reading literacy overall but suggested marginal differences for students with a low initial reading literacy. ## 14. Notes

### 14a. Bulleted notes
- The study uses observational data from NEPS (National Educational Panel Study, Starting Cohort 3) — not a designed experiment. Students self-selected into tutoring; groups were formed retrospectively.
- Treatment group: N=437 students who participated in ETG at some point during Grades 5–7; Comparison group: N=4676 who did not participate.
- The effect analysis uses the EffectLiteR multi-group SEM framework with covariate adjustment, not standard regression. The coefficients are unstandardized treatment effect estimates, not regression coefficients.

- Plausible values (PV) methodology was used to correct for measurement error in reading literacy, with 20 PVs drawn per student.

**Abstract:**
> This study examines the impact on student achievement of implementing a bundle of best practices from high-performing charter schools into low-performing, traditional public schools in Houston, Texas, using a school-level randomized field experiment and quasi-experimental comparisons. The five practices in the bundle are increased instructional time, more effective teachers and administrators, high-dosage tutoring, data-driven instruction, and a culture of high expectations. The findings show that injecting best practices from charter schools into traditional Houston public schools significantly increases student math achievement in treated elementary and secondary schools—by 0.15 to 0.18 standard deviations a year—and has little effect on reading achievement. Similar bundles of practices are found to significantly raise math achievement in analyses for public schools in a field experiment in Denver and program in Chicago.

§6

When this pattern fits

Good fit

  • Large corpora — dozens to hundreds of documents
  • Fields with a clear, codeable answer (numbers, categories, short facts)
  • Consistency across the whole corpus matters as much as speed
  • You can pilot on a sample and measure agreement before scaling

Poor fit

  • Open-ended qualitative synthesis with no fixed answer to reconcile toward
  • Corpora small enough that manual dual-coding is already fast (under ~20 papers)
  • No one available to write or maintain the codebook
  • No time budgeted for the reliability pilot in §2.6 — skipping it defeats the method
  • No plan for the token/compute cost at scale

On that last point: this isn't token-cheap. Double-coding plus reconciliation across hundreds of papers runs a lot of model calls, and your billing plan changes how that lands. The case study in §5 ran on a flat-rate Claude Code subscription, which made cost predictable regardless of corpus size; on pay-per-token API billing, budget for it before committing to the full run.

The codebook: one Markdown file

Format is fixed: Markdown. Not JSON, not YAML, not a spreadsheet — the workers read it as instructions, the same way they read every other file in this folder. Start from template/SKILL_codebook.md, copy it to codebook/SKILL_codebook.md inside your project folder, and fill it in there. That copy — not the template in the unzipped folder — is what {codebook_path} in your CLAUDE.md points to.

Step by step

  1. Download the ZIP and unzip it anywhere on disk. No GitHub account, no git — just a folder.
  2. Create your project folder with the layout above: papers/, codebook/, output/.
  3. Copy the engine in — CLAUDE.md, .claude/agents/, and prompts/ from the unzipped folder into your project folder.
  4. Fill in the four path placeholders in your copy of CLAUDE.md: {project_root}, {input_dir}, {codebook_path}, {output_dir}.
  5. Drop your PDFs into papers/, one file per paper.
  6. Draft the codebook at codebook/SKILL_codebook.md — see below for briefing Claude to write the first draft.
  7. Open Claude Code at your project root. It needs CLAUDE.md and .claude/agents/ present to auto-load the subagents.
  8. Run the three stages, each in a fresh session: paste "Read prompts/01_REVIEWER_A_ORCHESTRATOR.md and follow it," then the same for stage 2 and stage 3. HOW_TO_RUN.md has the full detail — parallelism tuning, progress checks, what to do when a session stalls.

Let Claude draft the codebook — but brief it like an RA

This is §2.1 one level up: you're asking an LLM to write the LLM's own instructions, and the failure mode is identical. A vague ask produces a plausible-sounding but ungrounded codebook, for the same reason a vague codebook produces plausible-sounding but ungrounded extractions — nothing forces the model to ground itself in what your papers actually say.

Vague prompt

"Make a codebook for my systematic review on workplace mentorship programs."

  • No inclusion criteria to ground exclusion codes
  • No example papers — fields get invented from the topic name alone
  • No reference to the template shape, so the output may not match what the pipeline expects
  • No instruction to stop and check before scaling up

Specific prompt

"I'm running a systematic review on workplace mentorship programs and their effect on retention and promotion rates. Our inclusion criteria: [paste the real ones]. I've attached three papers from the corpus — an RCT, a quasi-experiment, and a descriptive study — so you can see the range of designs.

Using template/SKILL_codebook.md as the shape, draft codebook/SKILL_codebook.md: our inclusion/exclusion codes, our outcome taxonomy (based on what these three papers actually measure), our intervention typology, and a decision rule for every field — the same way examples/esd/excerpt.md shows one. Don't fill in real values yet. Show me the skeleton first; we'll pilot it against paper #1 before touching the rest of the corpus."

Pilot before you scale

Draft codebook → one-paper test → reliability pilot (§2.6) → full corpus. Don't skip from "Claude wrote a codebook" straight to running the whole corpus — read the one-paper output against the real text first, then check agreement on a small sample. That order is what turns a bad first draft into one correctable paper instead of three hundred wrong ones.
